<<< QUESTION 1 >>>

Explain how Stable Diffusion generates an image from a text prompt. Describe the roles of the text encoder, latent space, noise, denoising process, and decoder. Use an example relevant to Walmart.

### WRITE ANSWER BELOW ###

Stable Diffusion generates an image from a text prompt by running a denoising process in a compressed latent space, guided by the prompt at every step. 
 
Text Encoder: The prompt is passed through a pretrained CLIP text encoder, producing a sequence of contextual embedding vectors that capture the 
prompt’s meaning. These embeddings are injected into the denoising network via cross-attention at every single denoising step — not just once — 
so the model continuously “checks the prompt” as it refines the image.
 
 Latent Space: Rather than working directly on full-resolution pixels, Stable Diffusion uses a VAE (Variational Autoencoder) to compress a 
 real image x into a much smaller latent representation z (e.g., 512×512×3 pixels → 64×64×4 latent). All noising and denoising happens in this 
 compact z-space, which is what makes Stable Diffusion fast compared to pixel-space diffusion models.
 
Noise (forward process): During training, a real image’s latent z₀ is progressively corrupted with Gaussian noise over many steps until, at step T, 
it’s indistinguishable from pure random noise. 
At generation time, there’s no real image to start from — generation begins directly from pure random noise in latent space. 


Denoising process (reverse process): A UNet is trained to predict the noise added at each step, conditioned on both the current noisy latent and 
the text embeddings (via cross-attention). That predicted noise is converted into a score (direction toward higher probability/more realistic data), 
and the network iteratively removes noise step by step, walking from pure noise back to a clean latent that represents a realistic image — 
guided by the prompt throughout via classifier-free guidance (mixing the conditional and unconditional score predictions to control how strongly 
the output matches the prompt).

Decoder: Once the reverse process reaches a clean latent z₀, the VAE’s decoder upsamples it back into a full-resolution pixel image.

Example of Walmart:
Generating product lifestyle imagery from text — e.g., prompting “a modern grey sofa in a sunlit living room” to auto-generate catalog/marketing 
images showing the same furniture item staged in different room styles and settings, without needing a physical photoshoot for every variation.

Explain the effect of guidance scale and inference steps in Stable Diffusion. Why are these parameters important when generating product images for an e-commerce platform?

### WRITE ANSWER BELOW ###

Guidance scale:
In a classifier free guidance one diffusion model is trained such that it can act both conditionallyP(x|y) and unconditionallyp(x).
Now at a denoising step, the vague instinct which is unconditional nudges moderately towards clean image whereas conditional has a greater effect.

∇x log Pλ(x|y) = (1−λ)·∇x log P(x) + λ·∇x log P(x|y)

Here λ is the guidance scale. If λ=1 then it removed the unconditional part and recovers the plain guidance generation.
However when  λ>1 then it exaggarates the conditional signal trading some diversity for prompt fidelity.

Inference Steps:
The reverse/denoising process you learned: x_T → x_{T-1} → ... → x_1 → x_0, walking from pure noise down to a clean image, 
one small Langevin-style step at a time. 
Inference steps = how many of these denoising steps you actually run at generation time.

More inference steps → each denoising step is smaller/more gradual → finer, more precise refinement → better detail, fewer artifacts, but slower generation

Fewer inference steps → each step has to “jump” further → faster generation, but risk of incomplete denoising — blurry details, muddled textures, structural artifacts (e.g., warped shapes, incorrect proportions) because the model didn’t get enough gradual refinement passes to converge properly.

Why are these parameters important when generating product images:

Too few steps risks product images with blurry textures, malformed logos, or distorted shapes — unacceptable for a catalog image customers rely on to judge the actual product.

Too many steps adds unnecessary compute cost/latency when generating at scale (e.g., thousands of SKU images), with diminishing quality returns past a certain point (typically ~30-50 steps is the practical sweet spot for most modern samplers).

For e-commerce, we want enough steps to guarantee crisp, artifact-free detail (since product accuracy and visual trust matter directly to purchase decisions), balanced against generation cost at scale if you’re batch-producing images for a large catalog.

Guidance scale (λ): needs to be high enough that the generated image actually reflects the specific product described (correct color, correct logo placement, correct shape) rather than drifting into a generic/unrelated variation — fidelity to the product description is non-negotiable for retail listings.Okay
Inference steps: needs to be high enough to avoid visual artifacts/blur that would misrepresent the product’s actual appearance, balanced against the cost of generating at catalog scale.

In practice, guidance scale and inference steps must be tuned together, not independently — e.g., Stable Diffusion’s defaults (~7-8 guidance scale, ~30-50 steps) balance both concerns for most product photography use cases.

A Stable Diffusion model generates a product image for "a blue 1-liter insulated water bottle with a black cap," but the output contains a green bottle with a different cap. Why can this happen, and how could you improve the consistency of the generated image?

### WRITE ANSWER BELOW ###

This might have happened because of multiple factors.

Attribute binding failure (the most common cause of exactly this symptom): Diffusion models’ cross-attention mechanism sometimes fails to correctly bind an adjective to its specific noun when a prompt has multiple objects/attributes in one sentence.

Training data bias / prompt ambiguity: if “insulated water bottle” strongly co-occurs with green in the model’s training data (e.g., many popular insulated bottles are green/steel-colored in product photography datasets), the model’s learned prior can override a weaker color instruction

Seed/random variance: a single generation is one sample from a distribution — any individual sample can land on an atypical, incorrect-looking result purely from randomness in the noise jitter, even with good settings.

Low guidance scale produces vague/off-prompt images broadly.

Too few inference steps causes blur/artifacts/malformed shapes.

In order to improve the consistency.

Increase guidance scale (your point — still valid, e.g., 7-9) to strengthen prompt adherence generally.

Increase inference steps (your point — valid for reducing artifacts, e.g., 30-50), but note this fixes blur/malformation, not necessarily wrong attributes.

Prompt engineering for attribute binding: restructure the prompt to reduce ambiguity — e.g., explicit ordering, repetition, or separating clauses: “a 1-liter insulated water bottle, bottle body is blue, cap is black”

Use image-to-image / inpainting with a reference: for strict product accuracy (critical in e-commerce, as in your earlier answer), the more reliable production approach is often providing a reference product image (actual photo or 3D render) and using image-to-image conditioning or ControlNet (shape/color conditioning) rather than relying purely on text-to-image.

Design a high-level Stable Diffusion workflow for Walmart to automatically generate lifestyle images for thousands of products. Explain the major stages and mention two potential risks.

### WRITE ANSWER BELOW ###


Major Stages of the Workflow:
Product Data Ingestion: Pull structured product metadata from Walmart’s catalog system — title, category, color, material, dimensions, existing product photos (if any). This becomes the raw input for prompt construction, not something a human writes per-product.

Automated Prompt Construction: Programmatically generate structured prompts per product using a template that fills in subject/environment/composition/lighting/style from the metadata (your point, but automated rather than manual) — e.g., "{color} {material} {product_type}, placed in {environment template}, {composition template}, {lighting template}, {style template}". For thousands of SKUs, this must be templated/rule-based or LLM-assisted, not hand-written per item.

Reference-Conditioned Generation (not pure text-to-image): Since exact product fidelity matters (as established in your earlier answers), the workflow should use the existing real product photo as an image-to-image or ControlNet conditioning input, combined with the text prompt — rather than pure text-to-image, which risks wrong colors/shapes.

Batch Generation with Tuned Parameters: Run generation at scale with guidance scale (~7-9) and inference steps (~30-50) tuned.


Automated Quality Control / Filtering: Critical missing stage — run generated images through automated checks (e.g., a vision-language model or CLIP similarity score comparing generated image against the original product description/photo) to flag mismatches (wrong color, missing logo, distorted shape) before they ever reach a human reviewer or go live.

Human-in-the-loop Review: For flagged or borderline cases, route to a human reviewer rather than auto-publishing — especially important at launch, before trusting the pipeline’s QC fully.


Publish to Catalog / CDN: Approved images get pushed into the product listing pipeline.

Two Risks:
Product misrepresentation / legal-compliance risk: as explored in the bottle example, generated images can show incorrect colors, logos, or shapes that don’t match the actual shipped product. In retail, this isn’t just a quality issue — it creates real legal/return-rate/customer-trust risk

Scale/cost and latency risk: generating lifestyle images for “thousands of products” at high guidance scale + 30-50 inference steps is computationally expensive (GPU-hours at scale). Without careful infra planning (batching, model optimization, caching repeated backgrounds/environments), this could become a significant cost and turnaround-time bottleneck, especially if the catalog is large or updated frequently.
